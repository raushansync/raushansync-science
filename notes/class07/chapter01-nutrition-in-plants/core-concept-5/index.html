a. Intuitive Explanation

Making food is only the beginning. A deeper question follows naturally: Once food is made, how is it used and stored by the plant?

Plants do not consume all the food immediately after producing it. Life is continuous, but photosynthesis is not — it depends on sunlight. So plants must:

  • use some food right away,
  • store some for later,
  • and convert some into other useful substances.

Understanding this helps explain why plants can survive at night, during cloudy days, or even when conditions change.

b. Formal Idea

The food produced during photosynthesis is mainly a carbohydrate. A portion of this food is:

  • used by the plant for energy and growth,
  • and the remaining portion is stored, often in the form of starch.

Plants can also use this carbohydrate to make other food components such as proteins and fats.

This idea exists to show that food production is not an isolated event — it supports the entire functioning of the plant body.

c. Deep Explanation

Let us unpack this in three connected steps.

1. Immediate use of food

As soon as food is formed:

  • some of it is broken down,
  • energy is released,
  • and this energy supports life processes like growth and repair.

This happens continuously, even while food is being made. So photosynthesis and food usage often occur at the same time.

2. Storage of excess food

Plants often produce more food than they need at that moment. This extra food is:

  • converted into starch,
  • and stored in different parts such as leaves, stems, roots, fruits, or seeds.

Starch is used for storage because: it is stable, it does not dissolve easily in water, and it can be converted back into usable food when needed.

This storage ability allows plants to survive periods when photosynthesis cannot occur.

3. Conversion into other substances

Carbohydrates formed during photosynthesis contain: carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.

Plants use these carbohydrates as a base to make:

  • fats,
  • and, with the help of nitrogen, proteins.

Nitrogen is not obtained from food or air directly in usable form. It enters the plant through the soil, either naturally or through fertilisers.

This shows that food made during photosynthesis is not an end product — it is a starting material for building the entire plant body.

d. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: Plants store food only in fruits.

  • Why it feels correct: Fruits are visibly associated with food.
  • Why it is incorrect: Food can be stored in many plant parts, depending on the plant’s structure and needs.
  • Correct way to think: Storage location varies; the principle of storage remains the same.

Misconception 2: Starch is a different kind of food from carbohydrates.

  • Why it feels correct: Starch is often spoken of separately.
  • Why it is incorrect: Starch is a form of carbohydrate used mainly for storage.
  • Correct way to think: Starch is stored carbohydrate.

e. Conceptual Connections

This concept strengthens earlier ideas:

  • Photosynthesis explains how food is made.
  • Storage explains how food supports life over time.
  • Conversion explains how simple food becomes complex body material.

It also prepares us for understanding:

  • how scientists test whether photosynthesis has occurred,
  • and why starch presence is used as evidence of food formation.

  • What problem would arise if plants could not convert carbohydrates into other substances?
  • Why is starch suitable for storage but not for immediate transport?
